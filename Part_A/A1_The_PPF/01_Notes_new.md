### Opportunity Cost

[▶ Beat 6](#beat-2.a) Let's build up some intuition for opportunity cost with a little example. Imagine Molly, a small scale organic farmer, is good at growing spinach and carrots on her $100$ hectare farm. [▶ Beat 7](#beat-2.b) If she spends all her time growing spinach she can grow $40$ tons per year. [▶ Beat 8](#beat-2.c) If instead she spends all her time growing carrots, she can grow $10$ tons per year. If Molly were to switch from growing all carrots to all spinach, she would give up $10$ tons of carrots and instead get $40$ tons of spinach.

[▶ Beat 9](#beat-2.d) What is Molly’s opportunity cost for 1 ton of carrots (1C)? To find it, we set those two production values equal [▶ Beat 10](#beat-2.e) and simplify. [▶ Beat 11](#beat-2.f) And that's it. Molly’s opportunity cost of $1$ unit of carrots is $4$ units of spinach. [▶ Beat 12](#beat-2.g) For every $1$ unit of carrots she wants to grow, she’ll have to give up $4$ units of spinach. And we could simplify the other way and find that the opportunity cost of 1 ton of spinach is 1/4 tons of carrots. They’re just reciprocals.

The reason we set them equal is because they are the two values that we can produce on the same amount of land.

### Production Possibility Frontier

There is a more systematic way to think about Molly’s farm. Molly doesn't need to not choose either carrots OR spinach. She can choose any combination of the two crops. So let's examine a range of crop choices she can make.

[▶ Beat 13](#beat-3.a) Let's use an $x,y$ graph to plot how much of each crop model is harvesting as we move through a few crop choices. Let's use the horizontal axis to represent her carrot harvest and the vertical axis to represent her spinach harvest. We've already established that she can harvest $10$ tons of carrots and $0$ tons of spinach, [▶ Beat 14](#beat-3.b) or $0$ tons of carrots and $40$ tons of spinach.

[▶ Beat 15](#beat-3.c) Based on her productivity numbers, if Molly plants carrots on half her land and spinach on the other half, she would have half of $10$ tons of carrots and half of $40$ tons of spinach. Let's also plot that on our $x,y$ graph.

[▶ Beat 16](#beat-3.d) We can pick another point, splitting the land into $\frac{3}{5}$ carrots and $\frac{2}{5}$ spinach. We find this by dividing by 5 and multiplying by the numerator, so $10 \cdot\frac{3}{5} = 6$ carrots and $40 \cdot\frac{2}{5} = 16$ spinach.

 [▶ Beat 17](#beat-3.e) I’m going to run through a few more on screen. [▶ Beat 18](#beat-3.f) As as we do, we can more easily see that any carrot and spinach combination Molly chooses lies on a line which we call the Production Possibility Frontier (PPF).

Lets say Molly starts by growing all spinach and no carrots. How many tons of spinach does she have to give up if she wanted to grow 1 ton of carrots? This is the same question as asking how much do we have to move on the vertical axis if we move one unit on the horizontal axis? But this is the same calculation as with opportunity cost. [▶ Beat 19](#beat-3.g) The slope of the PPF tells us her opportunity cost. [▶ Beat 20](#beat-3.h) To harvest $1$ more unit of carrots Molly must harvest $4$ fewer units of spinach. [▶ Beat 21](#beat-3.i) The slope here is negative since we're giving one good up for the other, exactly what opportunity cost is capturing.

At this point you might wonder how Molly would choose how much of each to plant. Great question. That’s a great question we’ll return to. For now we're just going to find what's possible to set up the trade-offs involved in our later models.

### Attainability

[▶ Beat 23](#beat-4.a) Molly’s PPF shows us all the combinations of carrots and spinach that she can produce by using all the land and labor available to her. [▶ Beat 24](#beat-4.b) Is it ever possible to produce at a point just inside the PPF like this?

This is what we call ***inefficient***, since there's productive land that's gone unused. You might be thinking that maybe she had a good reason to leave it unused, like to make sure she gets her full 8 hours of sleep. We'll get to how Molly chooses how much of each crop to plant later in this semester. But just for the moment all we can say is that there is missed potential harvest.

[▶ Beat 25](#beat-4.c) We call the points on the frontier ***efficient*** since Molly is using all her land and labor to it’s fullest.

[▶ Beat 26](#beat-4.d) Now lets say Molly wishes to produce at this point. What do we know about *it*?

If this were possible for Molly to produce with her labor and land it would be on her PPF. This point is what we call **unattainable** since is larger than what she is able to produce on her frontier.

[▶ Beat 27](#beat-4.e) *Q2 | Feasibility*

*Suppose Hagrid wants to bake 30R and 20F in one day. Is this inefficient, efficient, or unattainable? Use a graph or algebra to justify your answer.*

It turns out Molly has been working $10$ hours per day, but realizes she's burnt out and needs to spend less time working. She decides to spend only $8$ hours per day on her farm, which means she is now only able to grow on $\frac{4}{5}$ of her $100$ hectare farm. With this new work schedule she can only farm $80$ hectares. [▶ Beat 28](#beat-5.a) Her productive capacity has dropped to either $8$ carrots or $32$ spinach.

This is fine if this is her preference and she can still make her ends meet. [▶ Beat 29](#beat-5.b) The rest of the points along the PPF have shifted in with the extreme points. At this point I would recommend convincing yourself that any combination of crops lies on this new PPF.

This may seem like bad news for Molly’s wallet. And it could have been. [▶ Beat 30](#beat-5.c) But it turns out that later that winter she comes across a new carrot harvesting technology that would double her carrot harvest but not improve her spinach harvest.

What happens to Molly’s PPF after this new technology? [▶ Beat 31](#beat-5.d) We’ll with this technology she's able to grow up to $16$ carrots while nothing has changed her productive capacity. We see a pivot out of the PPF.

So what’s happened here? A change in Molly’s labor input has changed the output of her farm. The frontier depends on the level of labor. The more labor, the more is possible. And in this case since there's less labor, less is possible. And in addition to labor, the PPF also captures changes in technology.

These changes in the PPF aren’t purely mathematical. [▶ Beat 32](#beat-5.e) What we’ve found is a region that used to be inefficient that is now unattainable. [▶ Beat 33](#beat-5.f) And a region that used to be unattainable that is now on the frontier.

### In Closing

[▶ Beat 36](#beat-6.a) The story here is that what Molly has available to her depends on how much labor she puts into her farm and the efficiency of her technology. If she works more, she can have more produce. If her technology is better, she can have more produce. This relationship is what one person can do on their own. We also use this to model the productive capacities of countries.

If this were the best one could do, the story would be pretty boring. And we'd be pretty close to finished with this class. But some foundational insights in the 1800s give us our first counter-intuitive punchline: [▶ Beat 37](#beat-6.b) we can actually do better. This profound insight means we can exceed the frontier without changing inputs or technology. [▶ Beat 38](#beat-6.c) Showing how we do this requires some mathematics and a small detour into 1800s economic history. And that's exactly where we're going in the next few classes.
