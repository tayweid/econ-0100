Most of the time we make choices that impact others. One day you might find yourself stranded by yourself on a desert island or Martian moon. But if everything is going to plan I’d imagine you’re not there right now. And just like me, when you need food you need other people. This interaction, precisely how my choices interact with the choices of others when there are stakes, that’s what economics considers. 

There are a few main tools we use socially to coordinate our choices. Money IS a big one. Prices at their best tell us something about the relative value of something. If I give you the option to buy a carrot for a dollar, you’ll consider how much you’d like the carrot and how much you value the dollar and it's other uses. And when you choose the one you like the most you’re saying something about their relative value to you in that moment. 

And in democracies, votes are another important tool. Given the choice between two mayors, I can choose the one with the tax policy or reform agenda I like the best. But I can't have both mayors at the same time. 

Coordination devices make these types of things possible. 

That’s the core of economics: decision making and coordination with tradeoffs. And there’s a bit of magic ahead, both in what coordination can do for us, but also in pointing out social dilemmas, where coordination can and often fails catastrophically. 

I entered economics thinking about how to solve climate change. If you’re at all interested in these types of questions, you’re in the right place. These videos are aimed at folks of all ages. Critical and numerate thinking is helpful but you don’t need a background in economics, math, or statistics.

This first series sets the stage for where we’re going, introducing a toy world we’ll play with in the videos to come. And the basic question to keep in mind is: how can we use the idea of trade offs to make those involved better off? 

You'll notice both preferences and scarcity aren't directly about money or votes. We'll use both as tools, but the tools aren't the landscape.
