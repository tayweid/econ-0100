#### Guilding

It turns out Molly isn’t the only farmer in the area. Another farmer, Andrew, operates another small scale organic farm next to Molly’s. He also is good at growing carrots and spinach. He can grow $4$ tons of carrots per year and $18$ tons of spinach per year on a similar $100$ hectare farm. Molly is also able to produce more of both goods, so she has the ***absolute advantage*** in both carrots and spinach. 

Molly and Andrew decide to form a local farmers guild, operating their respective farms but shipping their produce together. Let's say the guild decides that both farms will produce the same ratio of carrots and spinach. Dividing their farms equally between crops, Andrew will produce $2$ carrots and $9$ spinach, and Molly will produce $2.5$ carrots and $22.5$ spinach. This adds up to $4.5$ carrots and $31.5$ spinach. Under this regime, the guild's PPF is just a line running through the guild's extreme points $9$ carrots and $63$ spinach, just the extreme points for the two farms added together.

But Molly, having studied economics in college realizes that opportunity cost might be important to consider when choosing their crops. Like we did for Molly, Andrew’s opportunity cost of carrots is $\frac{9}{2}$ spinach. Again, this is visualized by Andrew's steeper PPF, meaning he gives up more carrots for each ton of spinach relative to Molly.

So Molly has a lower opportunity cost of carrots and Andrew has a lower opportunity cost of spinach. Molly has to give up $\frac{1}{9}$ units of carrots for every $1$ unit of spinach, and Andrew has to give up $\frac{2}{9}$ units of carrots for every $1$ unit of spinach. So Andrew is relatively less efficient at growing spinach because he’s giving up more carrots to grow it. And Andrew, therefore, is relatively more efficient at growing carrots since he's giving up less spinach to grow it. This is the flip side of the opportunity cost equation we used at the beginning of the video. Andrew's next best use of his land is less costly when growing carrots even though Molly is better at growing carrots. This is because she is *much* better at growing spinach than Andrew. This is one of those important and somewhat unintuitive idea's you'll run into periodically in economics.

Maybe pause here and convince yourself that even though Molly is better at growing both goods, Andrew has a lower opportunity cost of growing carrots than Molly. He is gives up less than she does to grow carrots.



#### Comparative Advantage

Having a lower opportunity cost is what we call **comparative advantage**. And here, Andrew has a comparative advantage in carrots, and Molly has a comparative advantage in spinach. As you might have seen before, there is an important mathematical realization here. When one farmer’s opportunity cost is lower in one crop, the other farmer’s opportunity cost will always be lower in the other.

So Molly realizes that maybe there’s something to this idea, that maybe the guild should be minimizing their joint opportunity cost. So she and Andrew decide to specialize in what each farmer is relatively most efficient at growing, what they have a comparative advantage in. So Molly grows only spinach, growing $45$. And Andrew grows only carrots, growing $4$.

And we can check our work here by checking what happens to our production when we specialize in the good we don't have the comparative advantage in. And our production point is inside the guild's equal-split PPF, so we've correctly chosen the specialization according to comparative advantage. 

So without changing any technology, simply by choosing their crop according to their comparative advantage, the local farmers guild was able to exceed the frontier. Its a more general truth that so long as we're not too close to the edges, we can exceed any point on the PPF with specialization. The limit to this is when one farmer produces all of the good they don't have a comparative advantage in.

Why is this true? We’ve found the tradeoffs underlying the farmers’ crop choices, and we chose crops that minimize costs. Andrew has a lower opportunity cost of carrots. He gives up less to grow carrots, and Molly gives up less to grow spinach. So specializing in the lower opportunity cost crop makes the guild do better than a simple split.

This tells us something deep about opportunity cost, that by making choices together that minimize opportunity cost can make Molly and Andrew better off jointly. But possibly you're still concerned that the GROUP is better off but one of the INDIVIDUALS in the group may not be. We were slightly concerned that maybe we were sacrificing the individual for the benefit of the group. So let's turn to the model proposed by David Ricardo in 1817 to explain how specialization and trade can make two countries better off.
