### 1 | One Market

{++ We’ve now met three kinds of markets: one seller, two sellers and many sellers. Today we put them side by side on one demand curve, with the same firms in each. ++}

<!--
|   |   |
| --- | --- |
| 1.a | Title Three Market Structures; subtitle “All firms are similar.” The header: D: P = 120 − Q over MC = 2q. |
-->

{++ The only thing that changes from one to the next is what a seller’s marginal revenue looks like. So the question for today: what happens to price and quantity as sellers are added? ++}

<!--
|   |   |
| --- | --- |
| 1.b | Three columns with arrows between them: Monopoly (MR_A = 120 − 2q_A), Duopoly (MR_A = 120 − 2q_A − q_B and MR_B), Competitive (MR = P). “What happens to price and quantity as sellers are added?” |
-->

### 2 | The Firm’s Problem

{++ Every one of these sellers solves the same problem, in the same five steps. Set marginal cost equal to marginal revenue to get a quantity. Take that quantity to demand to get the price. Take it to average total cost to get the cost per unit. Multiply out the profit. Then ask whether that profit is enough to stay. ++}

<!--
|   |   |
| --- | --- |
| 2.a | Title The Firm’s Problem. The five-step card, one step at a time. “Every seller solves the same problem.” |
-->

### 3 | Monopoly

{++ Start with one seller. The monopolist faces the whole market’s demand, so its marginal revenue falls twice as fast as demand. ++}

<!--
|   |   |
| --- | --- |
| 3.a | Title Monopoly; subtitle “One seller: Q = q_A.” The card shrinks into the right panel, steps grey. The market graph with D, MR, MC and ATC. “The monopolist faces the market’s downward-sloping demand.” |
-->

{++ Step one gives 30 units. Step two, up to demand, gives a price of 90. Step three, across from average total cost, gives 63.33 a unit. Step four: 26.67 of profit on each of 30 units is 800. ++}

<!--
|   |   |
| --- | --- |
| 3.b | Step 1. 2q = 120 − 2q; 4q = 120; q\* = 30. A red dot where MR meets MC, 30 over q\*. “Every unit up to q\* adds more to revenue than to cost.” |
| 3.c | Step 2. Up to demand: P = 120 − 30; P\* = 90. “The price is what buyers will pay for q\* units.” |
| 3.d | Step 3. Across from ATC: ATC\* = 30 + 1000/30 = 63.33. “Average total cost: the cost per unit of making q\* units.” |
| 3.e | Step 4. Orange profit strips: π\* = (90 − 63.33)·30 = 800. “Profit: the price minus the cost per unit, on each of the q\* units.” |
-->

{++ Step five: with a profit, others would like to come in. But it’s a monopoly, so they can’t. ++}

<!--
|   |   |
| --- | --- |
| 3.f | Step 5. “No entry, because it’s a monopoly.” |
-->

{++ Is that the best the market could do? The efficient quantity is where marginal cost meets demand, at 40. The monopolist stops at 30. The units in between are worth more to buyers than they cost to make, and they don’t get made. That’s the deadweight loss. ++}

<!--
|   |   |
| --- | --- |
| 3.g.efficient | 2Q = 120 − Q; 3Q = 120; Q_sp = 40, in yellow where MC meets demand. “Q_sp: the socially efficient quantity, where marginal cost meets demand.” |
| 3.g | Grey strips between MC and demand, 30 to 40, named DWL. “DWL: every unit between q\* and Q_sp, from MC up to demand.” |
-->

### 4 | Duopoly

{++ Now two sellers. Step one is the same, marginal cost equals marginal revenue, but firm A’s marginal revenue depends on how much firm B sells. So step one gives a best response instead of a number. ++}

<!--
|   |   |
| --- | --- |
| 4.a | Title Duopoly; subtitle “Two sellers: Q = q_A + q_B.” The best-response graph. MR_A = MC_A; 120 − 2q_A − q_B = 2q_A; 120 − q_B = 4q_A; BR_A: q_A = 30 − ¼q_B, with its line. “Firm A’s marginal revenue depends on how much firm B sells.” |
-->

{++ Firm B solves the same problem with the letters swapped. ++}

<!--
|   |   |
| --- | --- |
| 4.b.symmetry | BR_B: q_B = 30 − ¼q_A and its line, by symmetry. “Firm B’s problem is the same, with A and B swapped.” |
-->

{++ Where the best responses cross, neither firm can do better by changing its own quantity. Each sells 24. ++}

<!--
|   |   |
| --- | --- |
| 4.b | A red dot at the crossing, named Nash Equilibrium. q_A\* = 30 − ¼q_A\*; (5/4)q_A\* = 30; q_A\* = q_B\* = 24; Q = 48. “Neither firm can do better by changing its output alone.” |
-->

{++ Step two: 48 units on the demand curve is a price of 72. Two sellers: more output, and a lower price. ++}

<!--
|   |   |
| --- | --- |
| 4.c | The market graph returns with the monopoly (30, 90) in grey. Step 2: 48 units up to demand; P = 120 − (24 + 24); P\* = 72. “Two sellers: more output, lower price.” |
-->

### 5 | Competitive

{++ Now many sellers, each too small to move the price. That takes a bigger market, so read the same demand line in thousands. ++}

<!--
|   |   |
| --- | --- |
| 5.a | Title Perfect Competition; subtitle “Many sellers.” Two graphs on one price scale: Firm (MC, ATC; units) and Market (D, S; thousands). “Competitive firms are what we call price takers.” |
-->

{++ There’s a step zero here: supply and demand set the price, and the firm takes it. At a price of 100, that price is the firm’s marginal revenue. ++}

<!--
|   |   |
| --- | --- |
| 5.b | Step 0. S: P = 5Q. 120 − Q = 5Q; 120 = 6Q; Q\* = 20; P\* = 5·20 = 100 = MR. The price carries across as the firm’s MR line. “Step 0: supply and demand set the market price.” |
-->

{++ From there it’s the same steps. Marginal cost equals 100 at 50 units. The price is 100. Average total cost is 70. Profit is 30 a unit on 50 units, 1,500. ++}

<!--
|   |   |
| --- | --- |
| 5.c | Step 1. 2q = 100; q\* = 50. “Every unit up to q\* adds more to revenue than to cost.” |
| 5.d | Step 2. P\* = 100 on the firm’s axis. “A price taker sells every unit at the market price.” |
| 5.e | Step 3. ATC\* = 50 + 1000/50 = 70. “Average total cost: the cost per unit of making q\* units.” |
| 5.f | Step 4. Orange profit strips on the firm graph: π\* = (100 − 70)·50 = 1,500. “Profit: the price minus the cost per unit, on each of the q\* units.” |
-->

{++ Step five is where competition differs. With profit and nothing keeping others out, firms enter. Supply pivots out and the price falls until the profit is gone, at the bottom of average total cost. ++}

<!--
|   |   |
| --- | --- |
| 5.g | Step 5. “With profit, more firms enter and the price falls.” Supply pivots out and the price rolls from 100 to 63: q\* 50 → 32, profit closes to zero. Long run: P\* = ATC\*; π\* = 0. “π = 0. Long run equilibrium: no entry or exit.” |
-->

### 6 | More Sellers

{++ Why does adding sellers push the price down? Look at one seller’s marginal revenue. When it sells one more unit, the price falls a little on every unit it was already selling. The more it sells, the more that costs it. So its marginal revenue is the price minus its own output, and the gap between price and marginal cost is the size of the firm. ++}

<!--
|   |   |
| --- | --- |
| 6.a | Title More Sellers; subtitle “n similar sellers.” MR_A = 120 − 2q_A − q_B = P − q_A; MR = MC, so P − MC = q. A red outcome dot on demand, an orange MC dot below it, a bracket named P − MC, and a table starting with n = 1: 30, 30, 90, 30. “Each seller’s MR is the price minus its own output.” |
-->

{++ Add sellers and each one is smaller. Two sellers, three, seven, seventeen: more output, a lower price, and a price closer to marginal cost. ++}

<!--
|   |   |
| --- | --- |
| 6.b.two | n = 2: q 24, Q 48, P 72, P − MC 24. |
| 6.b.three | n = 3: 20, 60, 60, 20. |
| 6.b.seven | n = 7: 12, 84, 36, 12. |
| 6.b | n = 17: 6, 102, 18, 6. “More sellers: more output, a lower price, a price closer to marginal cost.” |
-->

{++ With many sellers, each one is so small that selling one more unit hardly moves the price. Marginal revenue is just the price. That’s the price taker we started with in E2. ++}

<!--
|   |   |
| --- | --- |
| 6.c | “Many sellers: MR ≈ P” under the table. “A firm changing its quantity has no real effect on the price.” |
-->

### 7 | Summary

{++ One demand curve, three outcomes. The fewer the sellers, the higher the price and the less that gets made. Market power leads to inefficient markets. ++}
