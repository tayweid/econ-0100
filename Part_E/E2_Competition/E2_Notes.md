## Competitive Markets

Competitive markets have many buyers, many sellers, low barriers to entry, selling a homogeneous good, with no externalities.

Firms in competitive markets are just one of many. Since competitive firms like this are very small relative to the market, their choices have no real effect on the market. The firm graph essentially gives us a zoomed in view on the demand curve. And because we’re so zoomed in, it doesn’t have any meaningful slope.

This means competitive firms are what we call price takers. Changing quantity does not move the price in the market in any meaningful way.

So what does one more unit add to revenue? It sells for the market price, and we still get the same price for all the other units. That means the additional revenue from selling one more unit is the price. For a competitive firm, marginal revenue equals price.

As will likely feel familiar at this point in the semester, this is an example of marginal thinking. Our best decision involves thinking on the margin, thinking about the more or one less. When we choose based on whether we should change in little steps instead of big chunks, we can add clarity our decisionmaking.

## Entry and Exit

At this price, the firm is making a profit, which is nice for them. But profit is nice for everyone. If you’re looking at a market where others are making profit, what would *YOU* do? If we zoom back out to the whole market we can think about what happens to equilibrium when the firms are making profit.

<!--
|   |   |

Keep the purple ATC dot visible and following the chosen quantity throughout the remaining animation, including at the minimum of ATC. Keep the blue MR dot at that same quantity on MR; when the dots coincide, show the purple center with a blue rim so both remain visible. |

The demand curve is just the willingness to pay of many people all added together. And the supply curve is the willingness to sell of many sellers all added together. We find equilibrium by intersecting the supply and demand curves. This is where the price for the firm comes from. But profit is nice for everyone, so new firms open up to get in on some of that profit. New firms means the supply curve pivots out. More firms are willing to sell at each price.

<!--
|   |   |
| --- | --- |
| 3.d.entry | Pivot supply outward around the origin: its slope falls from 10/3 to 4/3 as price falls from 100 to 80, market output rises from 30 to 60 thousand, and firm output falls to 40. Yellow note: “With profit, more firms enter, pivoting the supply curve outward.” |
-->

You’ll remember that a pivot out like this leads buyers and sellers to lower their price. And a lower price lowers profit for the firm. Firms will continue to enter until there is no profit left.

<!--
|   |   |
| --- | --- |
| 3.d.zero | Continue pivoting supply outward around the origin toward zero profit; price reaches about 63.25. Yellow note: “Firms continue to enter until price is low enough that there’s no more profit.” |
-->

When price is low, firms are making a negative profit, or what we call a loss.

<!--
|   |   |
| --- | --- |
| 3.d | Continue pivoting supply outward around the origin, lowering price to 50; the firm produces 25 and earns a loss. Yellow note: “When price is low firms make a negative profit, called a loss.” |
-->

Firms earning a loss will shut down in the short run if price is below their AVC at the best positive output. Shutting down means producing nothing this period. We still have to pay the fixed cost. Exiting the industry is a different decision: it lets us avoid those costs in future periods.

<!--

The firm needs to decide whether operating with a loss is better than shutting down and still paying this period’s fixed cost.

<!--

Here, the price is above average variable cost. By operating, the firm earns enough to cover its variable costs and part of its fixed cost. It loses 375 instead of the full fixed cost of 1000. So it keeps operating.

## Long Run

In the long run, if price is below the firm’s ATC, they’re making a loss in the long run, and they exit, shifting the supply curve in, and the price up.

<!--
|   |   |
| --- | --- |
| 3.f.exit | Exit pivots supply inward around the origin, increasing its slope; price rises from 50 to about 63.25. Yellow note: “With a loss, firms exit, pivoting the supply curve inward.” |
-->

And if the price is above the firm’s ATC, they’re making a profit, and new firms enter, shifting the supply curve out, and the price down.

<!--
|   |   |
| --- | --- |
|   | Hold the equilibrium picture; entry was shown earlier. No further supply cycle is built here. |
-->

Combined, the only long-run stable position is when price is equal to ATC and MC, which also corresponds with zero profit. This gives us the zero profit condition: In the long run, firms earn zero profit.
