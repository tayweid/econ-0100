## Competitive Markets

Competitive markets have many buyers, many sellers, low barriers to entry, selling a homogeneous good, with no externalities.

We’ve just built up a model of the costs of production for the firm. From last time, the two most important cost curves for our firm are Average Total Cost ($ATC$) and Marginal Cost ($MC$). 

<!--

Firms in competitive markets are just one of many. Since competitive firms like this are very small relative to the market, their choices have no real effect on the market. The firm graph essentially gives us a zoomed in view on the demand curve. And because we’re so zoomed in, it doesn’t have any meaningful slope. 

<!--

This means competitive firms are what we call price takers. Changing quantity does not move the price in the market in any meaningful way.

<!--
|   |   |
| --- | --- |

So what does one more unit add to revenue? It sells for the market price, and we still get the same price for all the other units. That means the additional revenue from selling one more unit is the price. For a competitive firm, marginal revenue equals price.

## Profit and Output

At this price, what quantity maximizes the firm’s profits? Profit is everything left over after we’ve paid our costs, which is TR - TC.

{++ Start with revenue. Every unit sells for the market price, so total revenue is just price times quantity. If we make 20 units at a price of 100, that’s 2,000. On the graph it’s this rectangle: the price tall and the quantity wide. ++}

{++ For total cost we use average total cost. ATC tells us what each unit costs on average, so total cost is ATC at our quantity, times that quantity. At 20 units ATC is 70, so total cost is 70 times 20, or 1,400. That’s a second rectangle, sitting inside the first. ++}

If we start with a small quantity, we wonder whether we should increase quantity.

{++ Think about just the next unit. What is it worth to us? It sells for the price, so it’s worth MR. What does it cost to make? That’s MC. At 20 units the next one brings in 100 and costs 40, so making it adds 60 to profit. So we make it. Then we ask the same question again. ++}

And as I hinted, profit will continue to rise until MR = MC, then it will fall.

{++ At 50 units the next one brings in exactly what it costs. Past 50, every unit costs more to make than it sells for, so each one takes a little profit away. ++}

<!--

-->

Back to our question: How much should the firm choose to produce? At MC = MR!

As will likely feel familiar at this point in the semester, this is an example of marginal thinking. Our best decision involves thinking on the margin, thinking about the more or one less. When we choose based on whether we should change in little steps instead of big chunks, we can add clarity our decisionmaking.

Why don’t we choose at the minimum of ATC? 

{++ Because we’re not trying to make each unit as cheaply as possible. We’re trying to make the most profit. Past the bottom of ATC, the average cost is creeping up, but each unit still sells for more than it costs to make, so we keep going until MR = MC. ++}

{++ Whether that best quantity earns a profit depends on the price. When the price is above ATC, we make a profit on every unit. Lower the price and our best quantity slides down the MC curve. When the price reaches the bottom of ATC, we break even. ++}

## {++ Loss and Shutdown ++}

When price is low, firms are making a negative profit, or what we call a loss.

<!--
|   |   |
| --- | --- |
| 3.d | The firm alone: price falls to 50 and quantity to 25, and the profit area becomes a red loss. “When price is low firms make a negative profit, called a loss.” |
-->

Firms earning a loss will shut down in the short run if price is below their AVC at the best positive output. Shutting down means producing nothing this period. We still have to pay the fixed cost. Exiting the industry is a different decision: it lets us avoid those costs in future periods.

<!--
|   |   |
| --- | --- |
| 3.e.avc | AVC appears below the price. “Is operating with a loss better than shutting down and paying the Fixed Cost?” |
-->

The firm needs to decide whether operating with a loss is better than shutting down and still paying this period’s fixed cost. 

> *There might be a better way of doing this with AVC directly, just saying that we have fixed costs either way. All we need to do is cover the Variable Costs with Total Revenue. But it’s pretty good as it is.*

{++ Look at what we pay either way. The fixed cost is this band between AVC and ATC, and we owe it whether we make anything or not. Operating adds the variable costs underneath it, but it also brings in revenue. Here the revenue covers all of the variable costs and some of the fixed cost. ++}

<!--
|   |   |
| --- | --- |
| 3.e.fc | A grey fixed-cost band from AVC = 25 to ATC = 65. “The firm pays the full Fixed Cost even if it shuts down.” |
| 3.e.vc | A pink variable-cost rectangle below AVC. “Operating adds Variable Costs, but also brings in revenue.” |
| 3.e.revenue | A teal outline around Total Revenue. “Revenue covers all Variable Costs and part of the Fixed Cost.” |
-->

Here, the price is above average variable cost. By operating, the firm earns enough to cover its variable costs and part of its fixed cost. So it keeps operating.

{++ Put the two choices side by side. If we operate, we lose this much. If we shut down, there’s no revenue and no variable cost, and we lose the entire fixed cost. Operating loses less, so we operate. ++}

## {++ The Firm’s Supply Curve ++}

{++ So far the price has been handed to us. Now ask what this firm does at every price. Start the price at zero and raise it. At each price the firm picks the quantity where MR = MC, so its choices climb right up the marginal cost curve. ++}

{++ Below the bottom of ATC it operates at a loss. At the bottom of ATC it breaks even. Above that it makes a profit. But at every one of those prices, the quantity it chooses is on MC. That’s the firm’s supply curve: for each price, the quantity it’s willing to sell. The supply curve we’ve been drawing since Part B is marginal cost. ++}

{++ Does that depend on this firm’s particular costs? Here’s a firm with different costs, one whose average variable cost dips and comes back up. The curves look different, but the decision is the same. At a high price it makes a profit. At the bottom of ATC it breaks even. Between the bottom of AVC and the bottom of ATC it operates at a loss, because revenue still covers the variable costs and some of the fixed cost. ++}

{++ Below the bottom of AVC that stops being true. Revenue doesn’t even cover the variable costs, so operating loses the whole fixed cost plus the variable costs we couldn’t cover. Shutting down loses only the fixed cost. So below minimum AVC the firm produces nothing, and its supply curve drops to zero. ++}

## Entry and Exit

At this price, the firm is making a profit, which is nice for them. But profit is nice for everyone. If you’re looking at a market where others are making profit, what would *YOU* do? If we zoom back out to the whole market we can think about what happens to equilibrium when the firms are making profit.

{++ One thing before we let firms move. In the short run we’re stuck with our fixed cost, which is why ATC sits above AVC. In the long run nothing is fixed: the lease ends, the equipment can be sold. All costs are variable, so ATC is the curve that tells us whether this business is worth being in at all. ++}

The demand curve is just the willingness to pay of many people all added together. And the supply curve is the willingness to sell of many sellers all added together. We find equilibrium by intersecting the supply and demand curves. This is where the price for the firm comes from. But profit is nice for everyone, so new firms open up to get in on some of that profit. New firms means the supply curve pivots out. More firms are willing to sell at each price.

<!--
|   |   |
| --- | --- |
| 3.d.entry | Supply pivots out: price falls from 100 to 80 and the firm’s output to 40. “With profit, more firms enter, pivoting the supply curve outward.” |
-->

You’ll remember that a pivot out like this leads buyers and sellers to lower their price. And a lower price lowers profit for the firm. Firms will continue to enter until there is no profit left.

<!--

## Long Run

In the long run, if price is below the firm’s ATC, they’re making a loss in the long run, and they exit, shifting the supply curve in, and the price up.

<!--
|   |   |
| --- | --- |
| 3.f.loss | Supply pivots out to price 50. “When price is low firms make a loss.” |
| 3.f.exit | Supply pivots in and price rises to about 63. “With a loss, firms exit, pivoting the supply curve inward.” |
-->

And if the price is above the firm’s ATC, they’re making a profit, and new firms enter, shifting the supply curve out, and the price down.

<!--
|   |   |
| --- | --- |
| 3.f.exit | Hold; entry was shown earlier. |
-->

Combined, the only long-run stable position is when price is equal to ATC and MC, which also corresponds with zero profit. This gives us the zero profit condition: In the long run, firms earn zero profit.
