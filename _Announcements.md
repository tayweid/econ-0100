On Tuesday we will take a step back and consider how markets fit into the PPF framework we began with. Then refresh how the government gathers resources and influences behavior by imposing taxes on the sale of goods and services in a market. The major takeaways are:

- The government does not control which side of the market, consumers or producers, ends up paying the tax. These things are determined by the nature of the supply and demand curves in the market.
- The reduction is surplus value caused by a tax is generally larger than the amount of revenue raised by a tax. Minimizing this excess burden of the tax, however, would result in a tax system that many would find unfair.

We'll then break into groups to examine how governments can alter the distribution of the gains from exchange. The market divides the gains from exchange into consumer and producer surplus without any consideration of fairness or justice. It is possible for the government to redistribute the gains from exchange by setting a minimum or maximum price, but at a cost of reducing the overall levels of gain. We will look at three policies:

- A price ceiling which is designed to redistribute producer surplus to consumers. Rent control is a commonly used price ceiling.
- A price floor which is designed to redistribute consumer surplus to producers. A minimum wage law is a good example of a price floor.
- Government purchases of a good at above market prices is another way to redistribute surplus value from consumers to producers. Agricultural price supports are often done this way.

On Thursday we will use the supply and demand model to examine government trade policy. We will find that the market model suggests that

- Opening a country to trade creates winners and losers, and the winners gain more than the losers lose.
- Restricting trade by imposing import tariffs or quotas redistributes surplus value and decreases the overall amount of surplus value in the economy

This week we will look at market failure: situations where markets fail to maximize surplus value.

On Tuesday (CANCELED CLASS) we will begin by using the homework you’ve been working on to dig deeper into the concepts. Then we will introduce the concept of externalities. An externality is a spillover effect, a cost or benefit created by producing or consuming a good that impacts those not directly participating in the exchange. Externalities can be positive or negative. A good example of a positive externality is the choice to ride the bus instead of driving to work. Because the commuter decided to ride the bus, there is less congestion on the roads benefiting other drivers and there is an open parking space benefiting someone else who drove their car. A good example of a negative externality is carbon dioxide pollution from burning fossil fuels. The solution to this problem is to get the people creating the externality to take the costs or benefits their actions create into account when they make their choices. Economists call this internalizing the externality.

On Thursday we will hold our second MiniExam. As usual, we will begin class with some of your questions, sit for the MiniExam, then talk about it afterward. If you’ve studied the textbook, put in the time on the homework, and participated in class, you should feel confident in your knowledge and abilities.

Lastly, the next set of tutorials will be posted this week, looking at two situations where markets fail: externalities and public goods. Public goods are goods or services with some unusual characteristics: 1) the good or service is not excludable and 2) it is not rival. Excludability is the the property of being able to prevent someone else from accessing the good or service. This is the bases of private production: a firm can sell a goods or services because they can prevent you from consuming the good or service if you do not pay for it. If a good or service is not excludable, people may consume the good or service without having to pay for it and the market will not be able to produce the optimal amount. If a good is not rival it means that one person's consumption of the good or service does not reduce another person's ability to consume the same good or service. GPS location services are a good example of a service that is not rival. When goods or services are not rival, it makes no sense to limit the consumption of the good or service by charging a price for consumption.

On Tuesday we'll take MiniExam 4, which covers costs and perfectly competitive firms. I've posted a page with materials and a full description.

On Thursday we'll continue building our model of Monopoly Firms, firms with no competition. The main idea is that both the demand curve and marginal revenue curves are downward sloping, where for perfectly competitive firms both D and MR were flat lines at the market price. We'll use this model to discuss welfare implications and policy options.

We'll then extend the model to look at branded goods. When firms sell differentiated products but who's products can be substituted for other similar goods, they aren't fully Monopolists but also aren't facing perfect competition. Think about restaurants in Oakland. Most places have their own brand, but you'd be willing to find another place to eat if your favorite spot doubled their prices. That's what we'll discuss, and it's probably my favorite model.

On Thursday we'll begin two classes covering our final topic: utility and an individual's tradeoffs. This is often called "The Theory of Consumer Choice". When you walk into a store with \$5 to spend, you're confronted with many ways to spend your money, some better than others. So how should you choose what to buy when you have tradeoffs? The answer to this question gives us a peek into the workings behind the demand curve. And if you're already thinking about opportunity cost, you're on the right track.
