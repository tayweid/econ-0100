## Episode B0 | *Coordination can be done through prices*

In Part A we introduced the seed that plants the forest of economics. The cost of a decision is the value of the thing you could have had instead. This idea is embeded within the model of what's possible, the PPF. The slope of the PPF captures the tradeoff as you give up some carrots and for some spinach. We found that one farmer will always have a lower opportunity cost in one good and the other farmer with the other good. We found that by simply making choices that minimize the opportunity cost in the system, specializing according to comparative advantage, we could exceed the unspecialized outputs of the farms without changing labor or technology. We also found that we could achieve this higher output by finding an exchange rate between the two farms' opportunity costs. 

This is extrordinary and difficult to overstate. This is why we do highly specialized things. We pick something we're good at and choose to not do everything. Instead, we rely on others for most things in our lives. This is the core reason we live in cities and not cabins in the woods. But we're left with some remaining questions. First, how do we facilitate coordination like this across large groups of people without needing to make calculations about opportunity cost and comparative advantage every time we want a carrot or a sandwitch? I don't want to try to pin down how much of my labor I need to offer Aldi every time I'm hungry. Also Aldi doesn't necessarily want my labor. They already have people to stock the shelves. Second, we haven't said anything yet about preferences! The PPF tells us what's possible, but how do we pick between the many possible points on the PPF? We need to know which one we like better by including something about prefferences. Third, we realized that even though we could improve both farmers by finding an exchange rate between their opportunity costs, not all exchange rates favored both farmers equally. Some were better for one than the other. So we need some measure who's benefitting. 

That's what we're going to start working on in Part B, using prices as a coordination device: prices.
