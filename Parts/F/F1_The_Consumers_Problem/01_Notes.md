## Part 1: Preferences and Utility

### Animation 1 | Preferences

First, lets define preferences. By preferences we mean how a person ranks the alternatives available to them. We can describe how someone might rank different goods using what we call preference relations.

**DEFINITION**. Preferences are a ranking of different combinations, or *bundles* of alternatives/goods/services/options.

We have three preference relations.

1. $a\succsim b$ says that $a$ is at least as highly ranked as, or weakly preferred to, $b$
2. $a \succ b$ says that $a$ is strictly higher ranked, or strictly preferred to, $b$
3. $a \sim b$ says that $a$ and $b$ are equally ranked, indifference

With these preference relations, we can articulate three assumptions about consumer preferences.

### Animation 2 | Primitives

1. Completeness: Any $a$ and $b$ can be ranked by $\succsim$, $\succ$, or $\sim$.
2. Transitivity: If $a \succsim b$ and $b \succsim c$, then it must be that $a \succsim c$
3. Non-Satiation (More-Is-Better): More of a good always makes consumer at least weakly better off. Fairly plausible, if assume they can dispose of unwanted goods for free.

### Animation 3 | Indifference Curves

Given these assumptions, we can use a tool called indifference curves (or I-Curves) to summarize an individual's preferences over different combinations of two goods. Consider two goods, $X$ and $Y$. Suppose we are at a point $e$, which is just a bundle of $10 x$ and $10 y$.

What do our assumptions about preferences tell us about how the consumer ranks bundle $e$, relative to other bundles like $f$? Since we've assumed more is better we know that everything to the north east of $e$ is preferred to $e$, whereas everything to the south east is worse than $e$.

What about to the north west? This involves giving up some $X$, but getting some more $Y$. If at a point like $c$, the consumer gets just the right amount of extra $Y$, so that the extra pleasure from $Y$ exactly makes up for the lost pleasure from fewer $X$, we could expect the consumer to be indifferent between $c$ and $e$. Indeed, there could be many bundles between $c$ and $e$ that make the consumer just as well off as $e$ or $c$, because we are giving the right amount of extra $Y$ to make up for the lost $X$. Similarly, moving to the SE from $e$, the consumer loses $Y$ but gains $X$, and there will be a set of bundles that are equally good to $e$. If we connect all of the bundles that give the same pleasure as $e$, we get what is called an indifference curve.

**DEFINITION**. An *Indifference Curve* shows the set of bundles of two goods between which a consumer is indifferent.

If we pick any given bundle, we can imagine that there are bundles to the NW and SE that give the same amount of pleasure, and connect these with another indifference curve. 

A consumer has infinitely many indifference curves, forming the consumer's preference map.

### Animation 4 - 7 | Properties of Indifference Curves

1. Bundles on I-Curves further from the origin are preferred to those on I-Curves closer to the origin (more is better)
2. Every bundle lies on an indifference curve (completeness)
3. Indifference curves cannot cross (transitivity)
4. Indifference curves slope downward (more is better)
5. Indifference curves cannot be thick (more is better)

### Animation 8 | Utility

To succinctly summarize a consumer's preference map, economists use what is called a utility function. A utility function assigns a number to every indifference curve, representing what we call the utility of that set of bundles. The way it assigns these numbers summarizes the person's preferences. And this tells us that if a person likes bundle $a$ more than $b$, the utility function assigns a higher utility number to $a$ than to $b$.

**DEFINITION**. A *Utility Function* is a function such that if $a \succ b$, then $u(a) > u(b)$.

In this way the utility function summarizes the preference map, assigning a number to every possible bundle of goods, assigning higher numbers to bundles that the person likes more, capturing the person's preference ranking.

### Animation 9 | Marginal Rate of Substitution

The point of our model is to capture how people make choices involving trade-offs. So we want the model to capture how an individual wants to trade off one good for another. i.e., how many $x$ are they willing to give up, to get more $y$.

**DEFINITION**. A person's marginal rate of substitution ($MRS$) is the maximum number of units of the $y$-axis good the person is willing to give up to get one more unit of the $x$-axis good.

The $MRS$ is intimately related to the consumer's indifference curve. In fact, the $MRS$ is the just slope of the I-Curve. It tells us how much $y$ we need to give up to get more $x$ so we don't change how well off we are.

## Part 2: Budget Constraints

### Animation 10 | Budget Constraints

What else do we need to know to understand actual bundle choices? We've said that people choose the best bundle available to them, and the notion of "best" is pinned down by their indifference curves. So next we need to define what's available to them, what we call the choice set, which just the set of things the person can afford.

**DEFINITION**. Assuming that individuals can't borrow or save, the budget constraint is the price times the quantity for each good. 

$$Y = p_A q_A + p_B q_B$$

Saving is important and we have great models for it. But maybe surprisingly it's pretty complex, so for now we'll just say that people don't save.

Going back to our two good model with $A$ and $B$, lets set prices at $1$ for both goods and give the person $15$ dollars to spend. The budget constraint then looks like this. Any point on or inside the line represents the choice set, and any point on the line represents full expenditure.

Would we ever choose an allocation that is in the opportunity set, but not on the budget line? Well, since we're not saving, then no. More is better, or at least for most cases isn't worse.

This means out choices will always be on the budget line. So if we want to get more $A$ we need to give up some $B$. And how much we need to give up is simply just the slope of the budget line.

### Animation 11 | Marginal Rate of Transformation

**DEFINITION**. Marginal Rate of Transformation ($ MRT $) is the slope of the budget line.

The $ MRT $ is the tradeoff that the market imposes on individuals through prices. The slope of the budget line is just the rise over run, which we can write in the following way. 

$$ MRT = \frac{dq_2}{dq_1} = -\frac{p_1}{p_2}  $$

Here should be something about how changes in prices actually reduces the affordable set. If I only wanted X and the price of X went up, I would need a higher income to be able to afford it. Doing it this way will set up the income and substitution effects later.

In the next video, we'll bring the idea of the budget constraint to indifference curves and figure out how we maximize our preferences while constrained by what's possible. And in the process, we'll rediscover the law of demand.

## Part 3: The Consumer’s Problem

## Animation 1 | The Consumer’s Problem

Now that we have indifference curves, utility functions, the choice set, and the budget constraint, we can now start modeling what we often call "The Consumer's problem". 

Lets think about the problem visually. There's important mathematics behind the scenes that you'll learn if you study this more. 

But for now lets imagine an indifference curve with points lying below the budget constraint. Lets just pick a point on the indifference curve that lies within the choice set. 

We can move this point slightly closer to the budget constraint, remain in the choice set, and do slightly better than before. 

And remember the marginal rate of substitution happening in the background. If we were to vary the bundle slightly in the region of the original point, the MRS would also vary. Since there's a higher indifference curve running through this new pink point, lets bump up our utility so that the indifference curve runs through this new point. 

But again, there's still a small region between the indifference curve and the budget line that we can move our point into. And again we have some wiggle room for the MRS. 

At some point though, we run out of space for new more preferred points in the choice set and wiggle room for the MRS, and all we're left with is one point of tangency between the budget constraint and some indifference curve. This point happens on the budget line when the marginal rate of substitution is equal to the marginal rate of transformation.

1. $MRT = MRS$
2. $Y = p_A q_A + p_B q_B$

To stay on the budget line we need to give up some $A$ to get more $B$. And at this point we are indifferent between the amount of $A$ we have to give up and the amount of $B$ we would be getting instead.  

Here we've glossed over some important details, but this is the idea for the consumer's problem! And you'll notice the similarity with the basic idea in this class: that we want to find the point where the marginal benefits equal the marginal costs.
