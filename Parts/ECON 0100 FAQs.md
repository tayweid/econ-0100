### Week 4 | Government and Welfare

*Thanks for everyone who came to office hours this week. These are a few of the questions (in my words) we talked about:*

Q. Is the minimum wage an example of a price floor? And how? 

A. Yes! It's a funny one though, which is why we didn't use it in class on Thursday. Labor hours are what's bought and sold in a labor market: for labor, firms are the buyers and individuals are the sellers. This means demand comes from firms and supply comes from individuals. So ... a (binding) price floor means we get a price higher than in equilibrium, giving us a surplus. In labor market terms, this means too much labor is available with not enough jobs.

Q. What are examples of an inelastic and elastic good? 

A. One example of an inelastic good is something like food. Basically no matter the price of food, you're still going to buy it. You need it to survive. The same goes for many types of medical goods, like insulin. You need it to live, so you'll pay for it no matter the price. On the flip side, luxury goods, non-necessary and often expensive goods, are relatively more elastic: if you double the price of a fancy car, you'll see a much larger change in quantity demanded than you would if you were to double the price of food.

Q. What happens when a price ceiling is set above equilibrium (a non-binding price ceiling)?

A. A price ceiling sets the upper-bound on the price. The way I think about it is that the price ceiling tells us a range of prices that are off-limits: prices above some level aren't allowed. But if we don't make the equilibrium price off-limits, then we'll just end up at equilibrium like we would have without the price ceiling. This is what we call a non-binding price ceiling. And they don't affect the equilibrium. Price controls only influence the equilibrium when they exclude the equilibrium from the range of allowable prices (a binding price control).
