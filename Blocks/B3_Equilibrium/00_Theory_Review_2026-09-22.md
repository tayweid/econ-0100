## The argument B3 needs to establish

1. **Hold the market fixed and try a price.** Keep preferences, costs, and the population unchanged. For the competitive model, people choose quantities taking the common price as given. The good is the same across sellers; an alternative seller's spinach is a substitute.
2. **Use the decisions already built in B1 and B2.** Demand answers how much buyers would buy at that price; supply answers how much sellers would sell. These are desired quantities, not two different counts of completed transactions. Actual purchases necessarily equal actual sales, even away from equilibrium.
3. **Ask whether those choices fit together.** If buyers want 45 and sellers want to sell 20, all those plans cannot be fulfilled. At 40 and 40 they can. That compatibility, together with each side choosing its preferred quantity at the price, is the competitive equilibrium condition.
4. **Explain the pressure away from other prices.** With a shortage, an unserved willing buyer can gain by bidding more; with an excess, an unsold willing seller can gain by accepting less. Show the person, the available alternative, and the gain. This motivates price adjustment. It does not require tracing every subsequent trade.

The distinction in steps 3 and 4 matters. Identifying an equilibrium and explaining a process that might reach it are different tasks. B3 can teach both without giving them the same definition.

## Theoretical distinctions to preserve in the revision

**Available improvement versus wishing for a better outcome.** “No one wants to change” needs its choice set. A buyer can wish spinach cost less at equilibrium. The useful question is whether an available choice would improve their outcome. In the competitive model, the quantity choice is optimal given the price and the market clears.

**Bargaining versus competitive equilibrium.** Scene 2's $6.25 result establishes that Gary cannot profitably outbid under the displayed rules. The outline itself notes that Molly could ask Amanda-Grace for more. Calling this the full equilibrium concept leaves a missing assumption. Keep the bidding example, but consider postponing the full definition until the common-price market, or explicitly naming the limited switching test. This is a proposed revision to the approved scene-2 naming decision, not an instruction to silently change it.

**A threshold versus an offer.** MB is the maximum willingness to pay; MC is the relevant cost threshold. They need not be actual bids and asks. Labeling the bars as limits preserves their B1/B2 meaning and avoids adding a bidding strategy merely to populate the screen.

**Some incentives versus everyone's interests.** The notes say both buyers and sellers have an incentive to raise prices during a shortage. An already served buyer would ordinarily prefer to keep the lower price. The mechanism needs an unserved buyer willing to outbid and a seller willing to accept; other buyers may respond to avoid losing their purchases. It does not need everyone to prefer a higher price. The excess case has the corresponding distinction.

**A displayed adjustment rule versus a result derived from choices.** The outline correctly specifies a teacher-stepped price line, but later says it stops at $4 “by itself.” The animation should represent the argument already made. Stopping a programmed line at the crossing does not establish convergence of a decentralized market process.

**Allocation versus aggregate quantities.** Assigning scarce trades to the highest-value buyers and lowest-cost sellers is an explicit convention in the outline. So is completing all feasible trades on the short side, which gives Qx = min(Qd, Qs). Retain these conventions where needed for continuity with B4, but do not make the existence of a shortage depend on knowing exactly who receives every lot. B4 can examine the welfare consequences of the allocation.

## What counts as progress

Keep the approved one-trade close-up and the small examples of bidding and switching. They make gains and alternatives visible. The immediate priority is the central argument in scenes 4–6, already first in the outline's build order. A full seven-scene rebuild should follow a successful short walkthrough of that argument.

A useful rehearsal has four checks:

- On a paused frame, the student can distinguish willingness to trade from completed trade.
- The student can identify an affected person and explain an available improvement before it is animated.
- At $4, the student can explain both the equality of quantities and why the excluded people do not want to trade at that price.
- On the pasty exercise, the student can reproduce the reasoning without the plaza or familiar characters.

These are proposed checks, not results from a classroom test. If they fail, revise the explanation or representation before adding motion. A shorter stop count is useful, but independent reasoning is the substantive outcome.
