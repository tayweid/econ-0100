Last time we introduced preferences as a first step to resolving where on the PPF to live, and we used the buyer’s Marginal Benefit to decide whether another purchase was worth making. The extra value above what they paid was their Consumer Surplus.

Every exchange has two sides: a buyer and a seller. Just like we asked Amanda-Grace how much she values chocolate, we can just ask the seller how much they would be willing to produce at a range of prices.

Remember Molly? She can sell the spinach she chooses to grow at the price in the market. ~~We’ll measure her harvest in tons per year, like we did in Part A.~~ {++For this example, let’s look at the spinach she grows to sell on one market day. Let’s say Molly is happy to grow ~~2 tons~~ {++20 kilograms++} of spinach at a price of \$4 per ~~ton~~ {++kilogram++}. This is Molly’s quantity supplied at \$4, which we can write as ~~$Q_s(4)=2$~~ {++$Q_s(4)=20$++}.

This point represents her ***Individual Quantity Supplied***: the quantity a seller is willing and able to sell at a given price.

You’ll notice that while this graph looks a lot like our PPF, it’s doing a very different thing. Instead of plotting spinach against carrots, we’re plotting spinach against price.

Why doesn’t Molly produce one more? She could definitely sell more if she wished.

Because selling more is only worth doing if the extra spinach brings in enough to cover what it costs her. At this price, she stops at ~~2 tons~~ {++20 kilograms++}. Producing a little more would cost more than she would receive for it.

What happens when the price of spinach goes up? More of that extra production becomes worth doing. Molly can devote more land and labor to spinach, giving up their other uses. Let’s say she would like to produce ~~4 tons~~ {++40 kilograms++} of spinach at a price of \$6{++ per kilogram++}. We can plot this on the PQ graph too.

> *Show more of her farm devoted to spinach, and the point ~~(4, 6)~~ {++(40, 6)++} on the same price-quantity graph.*

As the price of spinach has gone up, Molly has increased her quantity supplied of spinach since it has become relatively more valuable. What would happen if the price drops, say to \$2? The quantity of spinach Molly would supply would go down because spinach is relatively less valuable than other uses of her land and labor. In this example, at \$2 she would produce none.

We can keep asking this question at different prices, just like we did with Amanda-Grace. Each answer gives us another point.

When we represent this relationship with a curve we call it the ***Individual Supply Curve***. This allows us to answer the question of how much Molly would sell at any price.

> *Show the definition of Individual Supply Curve.*

This positive relationship between prices and quantity is called the ***Law of Supply***.

> ***Law of Supply***: *The quantity supplied of a good rises with its price.*

Like demand, supply does not have to be a straight line. But we’ll use a linear relationship to keep the math simple. Molly’s supply curve in this example is ~~$P=2+Q_s$~~ {++$P=2+\frac{Q_s}{10}$++}.

Having an equation is handy. We can ask about lots of prices. How much spinach would Molly produce at \$5 per ~~ton~~ {++kilogram++}? Start at \$5 on the vertical axis, move across to her supply curve, and then down to the quantity axis. We find ~~3 tons~~ {++30 kilograms++}.

We can also ask the question the other way. Suppose Molly is already producing ~~1 ton~~ {++10 kilograms++}. What would it cost her to increase production a little? Start at ~~1~~ {++10++} on the quantity axis and read up to the supply curve. Its height is \$3 per ~~ton~~ {++kilogram++}. That tells us the cost per ~~ton~~ {++kilogram++} of adding a little more spinach at that point.

This is what we call ***Marginal Cost***: the cost of producing one additional unit.

Marginal still means “one more.” Because spinach is divisible, we can make that addition small. The curve tells us how costly it is to add more at the quantity Molly has already reached. It does not tell us the total cost of everything she has grown, or the average cost per ~~ton~~ {++kilogram++}.

Now lets go back to our original price of \$4. At ~~1 ton~~ {++10 kilograms++}, her marginal cost is \$3 per ~~ton~~ {++kilogram++}. Is producing a little more worthwhile? Yes: the next small addition brings in more than it costs. At ~~1 ton~~ {++10 kilograms++}, the additional spinach she produces costs her less than the \$4{++ per kilogram++} it brings in. At ~~2 tons~~ {++20 kilograms++}, the marginal cost exactly equals \$4 per ~~ton~~ {++kilogram++}. Beyond ~~2 tons~~ {++20 kilograms++}, it costs more than it brings in. That’s why she stops where marginal cost meets the price.

> *Hold price at \$4. Start at Q = ~~1~~ {++10++}. Sweep along supply toward Q = ~~2~~ {++20++}, showing MC below price, equal to price, then above price. Return to Q = ~~2~~ {++20++}.* *Read MC = \$4 from the graph at Q = ~~2~~ {++20++}; no second substitution.*

So not only does the supply curve tell us Molly’s quantity supplied, it also tells us Molly’s marginal cost. This is the same idea as with the Marginal Benefit in the Demand curve.

This way of seeing costs still uses opportunity cost behind the scenes. Growing more spinach takes land and labor away from other uses. We’re now measuring the value of what Molly gives up in money instead of carrots. That includes her own time, even when she doesn’t write herself a paycheck.

This tells us that Molly’s supply curve *is* her ***Marginal Cost Curve***: the cost of adding more spinach at each quantity.

#### Closing

Prices offer one coordination device to facilitate exchanges between buyers and sellers. But to answer the big questions we have remaining, we first need to pin down where prices *come from*.

Now we have both sides. Demand tells us how much buyers want to buy at a price. Supply tells us how much sellers want to sell at that same price. Next time we’ll put the two together and ask whether there’s a price at which those quantities agree.
