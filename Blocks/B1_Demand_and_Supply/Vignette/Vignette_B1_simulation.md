## Vignette B1 | Simulation | Chocolate Bars

*For the teaching team. Run the simulation first; students fill in Q1 and Q2 as you go, then work Q3 and Q4 on their own.*

**Bring:** a crate of basic chocolate bars.

**Framing.** We've avoided the question of how to choose where on the PPF to live. Prices can answer that question, giving us a device to coordinate choices across buyers and sellers.

**First,** have one student come to the front. Then ask them how many candy bars they would be willing to buy for smaller and smaller prices. Plot their individual quantity demanded at a range of prices.

Ask in this order, and have the room record each answer in the Q1 table:

- How many chocolate bars would you be willing to buy at \$4? That's a pretty steep price for just a single bar. Expect zero. This tells us their quantity demanded at \$4 is 0. Plot it on the price quantity graph.
- How many at \$2? This is a little more reasonable. Expect 1 bar. Plot this point too.
- How many at \$1? A slightly better price than the store. Expect 2. One for now, one for tomorrow.
- Keep going through a range of prices (\$0.50, \$0.25, \$0.10), asking how many they would buy at each. As the price becomes very low, expect them to buy many more than they actually want to eat in the moment, giving some to friends in class and holding onto some for later.

Each of these points represents an Individual Quantity Demanded: the quantity a buyer is willing and able to buy at a given price. Combined they form the Individual Demand Curve, or simply Demand. The line has a negative slope, which tells us that the buyer's preferences for chocolate bars satisfy what we call the Law of Demand: a good's quantity demanded falls with its price.

**Second,** have the rest of the class come to the front and arrange themselves in order of willingness to pay for the candy bar. Don't plot this but just talk about how the room is ordered from the highest willingness to pay to the lowest.

**Third,** but this isn't exactly right since at the lowest price, the highest willingness to pay might want to buy more than one. So start with a low price and find quantity demanded: at \$0.10, how many bars does the room want in total? Then raise the price and count again at \$0.25, \$0.50, \$1, \$2, \$4. The room records each count in the Q2 table and plots the points.

Adding up all the buyers gives us the demand curve, or the market demand curve.

**Hold back for Block B2.** Consumer surplus. If a student raises it ("I'd have paid \$4 but I paid \$2"), that's nice for them, and it's where we're headed next week.

## Answer key | Q3 and Q4

Demand: $P_b = 17 - \frac{1}{6} Q_d$, galleons and thousands of pasties.

- Vertical intercept: $Q_d = 0$ gives $P_b = 17$ galleons.
- Horizontal intercept: $P_b = 0$ gives $Q_d = 102$ thousand pasties.
- At $10$ galleons: $10 = 17 - \frac{1}{6} Q_d$, so $Q_d = 42$ thousand pasties.
- At $5$ galleons: $5 = 17 - \frac{1}{6} Q_d$, so $Q_d = 72$ thousand pasties.
- Change from $10$ to $5$: quantity demanded rises by $30$ thousand pasties. Price fell and quantity demanded rose, which is the Law of Demand.
