#### Molly's Supply Curve

To begin, instead of conceiving of opportunity cost in terms of goods given up, as in spinach for carrots, we're going to slip money in as the units of opportunity cost. Molly would need to give up carrots to grow more spinach and the carrots she would give up would have a monetary value if she were to sell them at the farmers market. Now instead of spinach carrying a cost of carrots, the cost of spinach is measured in terms of money. *If this seems a little funny, I see you. We haven't even talked about where prices come from. But for now, lets just say there is some money and that its valuable.*

We've redefined the opportunity cost of spinach as the money I have to give up to make it. This is what we call ***Marginal Cost***: the cost of producing one additional unit. You'll notice the resemblance to opportunity cost. We've just simply introduced money as the units. 

Molly has a marginal cost of producing spinach. We can just ask her how much she would be willing to produce at a range of prices. To produce more spinach, she would have to increase the land she would farm, which would itself require renting more land and equipment and hiring more help, which all requires more expense. Let's say Molly is happy to grow 1000 pounds of spinach at a price of $\$4$ per pound. This is Molly's quantity supplied at $\$4$ dollars, which we'll denote with little Q subscript S as a function of four dollars. Let's plot this on the XY plane with the price of $\$4$ on the vertical and 1,000 pounds of spinach on the horizontal. You'll notice that while this graph looks a lot like our PPF, it's doing a very different thing. Instead of plotting spinach against carrots, we're plotting spinach against price. 

So what happens when the price of spinach goes up? Molly was happy with what she was doing. But now maybe she wants to rent more land to grow more spinach, use some of the land previously devoted to carrots, and maybe hire someone to tend to the new spinach. All this together, after the price increase it makes more sense for her to increase her spinach crop by shifting resources from other uses. Let's say she would like to produce 1,500 pounds of spinach at a price of $\$6$. We can plot this on the PQ graph too.

As the price of spinach has gone up, Molly has increased her quantity supplied of spinach since it has become relatively more valuable. What would happen if the price drops, say to $\$2$? The quantity of spinach Molly would supply would go down because spinach is relatively less valuable than other uses of her land and labor. This positive relationship between prices and quantity is called the ***Law of Supply***. 

"***Law of Supply***: The quantity supplied of a good rises with its price." 

When we represent this relationship with a curve we call it the ***Individual Supply Curve***. This allows us to answer the question of how much Molly would sell at any price. Molly will increase her quantity supplied to the point where the cost of producing one more pound of spinach is equal to the price of a pound of spinach. This tells us that Molly's supply curve *is* her ***Marginal Cost Curve***: The cost of adding one additional unit of spinach, an idea we will return to later in the series.

#### Producer Surplus

Molly makes some spinach and sells it into a market at some price. How nice are this setup for her? Let's zoom in on one pound of spinach and add things up. If selling this one pound of spinach costs her $\$3$ and the price is $\$5$, there's an extra $\$2$ that she gets from this exchange above her  $\$3$ costs. This extra value to Molly is what we call ***Producer Surplus***, which is essentially the extra niceness from the exchange. 

If the price goes up, Molly's producer surplus from this exchange goes up. If the price goes down, Molly's producer surplus from this exchange goes down.

This is for one very specific pound of spinach. To measure all of Molly's producer surplus, we need to look at every pound of spinach produced and sold at this price. We can plot a vertical line representing the producer surplus for each of the items Molly produces at at price of $\$4$. The area covered by the producer surplus bars is the shape of a triangle. It's a bit funny, but since spinach doesn't have to be in distinct pound chunks, we can actually measure this area as a triangle. 

When price falls, producer surplus decreases for two reasons. First, the producer surplus for all the spinach produced is now smaller. Molly gets less extra value above her cost of producing the spinach she still produces at this lower price. And second, she actually produces less than before, which means there's less spinach that has producer surplus. 

The opposite is true when price rises. Producer surplus increases for both of these reasons: Molly get's more surplus value for all the spinach she was producing before *and* she produces more spinach which makes producer surplus.

#### Shifters

Molly's ***Individual Supply Curve*** depends on the costs of producing spinach. For example, what would happen if the price of renting farmland increased? Her supply curve would also increase! The cost of each additional unit of spinach becomes more expensive since the input, land, has also become more expensive. This means that, for example, she wouldn't be willing to produce as much spinach as before at a price of $\$4$ or any price. 

What would happen if she had to pay less for her organic fertilizer? The each additional unit of spinach would become less costly to produce. Molly would be willing to make *more* spinach than before at at price of $\$4$ or any price. 

What would happen if the price of carrots were to increase? Molly would have a higher opportunity cost of switching her farmland away from carrots to spinach, so the cost of production would also go up!

#### Market Supply

Since Molly isn't the only farmer growing spinach so we need to look at other farmers' individual supply curves as well. You remember Andrew, right? The farmer next door. He also has an individual supply curve. His individual supply curve is a little steeper than Molly's. This reflects the fact that it's more costly for him to increase his production of spinach than it is for Molly. We can find his quantity supplied at the three prices we used before by intersecting the prices with his individual supply curve. 

To account for the entire supply of spinach we need to include Andrew's individual supply curve. So lets take what he's growing at every price and add it with Molly's quantity supplied. When we add together all the farmers individual supply curves, we call this the ***Aggregate Market Supply Curve*** or sometimes simply ***Supply***. This shows how we horizontally sum the quantities of two individual supply curves, but we could do it for any number of sellers. 

We can represent this ***Supply Curve*** with an equation for a line that goes through the quantity supplied of 10 at a price of 4. This is the supply curve P, for price, is equal to 2 over 5 times Q, for quantity. Plugging in any value of P gives us the quantity supplied at that price. So for example a quantity supplied at a price of $2 would be 5,000 pounds of spinach. 

#### Producer Surplus Again

Just like Molly's Producer Surplus, we can find the producer surplus for the entire market by adding up the difference between the price and the cost of producing that pound of spinach. Doing this for every pound of spinach gives us the area of a triangle. This is the producer surplus in the market at this price, which is one way of measuring how good the exchanges in the farmer's market is for people like Molly.

#### Shifters Again

The changes in the costs of production shift the farmers' individual supply curves, which in turn shifts  the market supply curve. We'll come back to think about how shifters impact producer surplus, but we don't yet have all the tools. If the cost of inputs like farmland or fertilizer were to increase, production would become more costly for farmers, meaning the supply curve would shift up. Each quantity supplied is now more expensive and farmers are willing to sell less at any given price. 

The market supply curve is also impacted by the number of sellers. Like when we added Andrew's supply to Molly's to get the market supply curve, every time another seller opens their doors the supply curve shifts out, and when a seller drops out of the market the supply curve shifts in.
