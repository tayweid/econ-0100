#### Molly's Opportunity Cost

Let's build up some intuition for opportunity cost with a little example. Imagine Molly, a small scale organic farmer, is good at growing spinach and carrots on her $100$ hectare farm. If she spends all her time growing spinach she can grow $45$ tons per year. If instead she spends all her time growing carrots, she can grow $5$ tons per year. If Molly were to switch from growing all carrots to all spinach, she would give up $5$ tons of carrots and instead get $45$ tons of spinach. So we’ll set those two values equal and simplify. And that's it. Molly’s opportunity cost of $1$ unit of carrots is $9$ units of spinach. For every $1$ unit of carrots she wants to grow, she’ll have to give up $9$ units of spinach. This idea is crucial to what we'll do later.

This idea is crucial to our later models. The next of which will level this idea up into what we call the Production Possibility Frontier. 



#### Molly's Production Possibility Frontier

There is a more systematic way to think about Molly’s farm. We've gone through and calculated Molly's opportunity cost for her farm. But Molly doesn't need to not choose either carrots OR spinach; she can choose any combination of the two crops. So let's examine a range of crop choices she can make. Let's use an $x,y$ graph to plot how much of each crop model is harvesting as we move through a few crop choices. This lets us use cartesian coordinates to understand behavior! Let's use the vertical axis to represent her carrot harvest and the horizontal axis to represent her spinach harvest. We've already established that she can harvest $5$ tons of carrots and $0$ tons of spinach, or $0$ tons of carrots and $45$ tons of spinach. 

Based on her productivity numbers, if Molly plants carrots on half her land and spinach on the other half, she would have half of $5$ tons of carrots and half of $45$ tons of spinach. Let's also plot that on our $x,y$ graph.

We can pick another point, splitting the land into $\frac{3}{5}$ carrots and $\frac{2}{5}$ spinach.

As we run through more points its easy to see that any carrot and spinach combination Molly chooses lies on a line which we call the Production Possibility Frontier (PPF). Take a look at the range of other combinations. And guess what? Calculating the the slope of this relationship gives us her opportunity cost. To harvest $1$ more unit of carrots Molly must havest $9$ fewer units of spinach. The slope here is negative since we're giving one good up for the other, exactly what opportunity cost is capturing. This is really the trade-off captured by the opportunity cost and as we mentioned earlier it's embedded in the PPF relationship.

#### Coordination

Most of the time we make choices that impact others. One day you might find yourself stranded by yourself on a desert island or Martian moon. But if everything is going to plan I’d imagine you’re not there right now. And just like me, when you need food you need other people. This interaction, precisely how my choices interact with the choices of others when there are stakes, that’s what economics considers. 

There are a few main tools we use socially to coordinate our choices. Money IS a big one. Prices at their best tell us something about the relative value of something. If I give you the option to buy a carrot for a dollar, you’ll consider how much you’d like the carrot and how much you value the dollar and it's other uses. And when you choose the one you like the most you’re saying something about their relative value to you in that moment. 

And in democracies, votes are another important tool. Given the choice between two mayors, I can choose the one with the tax policy or reform agenda I like the best. But I can't have both mayors at the same time. 

Coordination devices make these types of things possible. 

That’s the core of economics: decision making and coordination with tradeoffs. And there’s a bit of magic ahead, both in what coordination can do for us, but also in pointing out social dilemmas, where coordination can and often fails catastrophically.
