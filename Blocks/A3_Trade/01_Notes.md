### Specialization and Trade

*Now we turn to finding one. Can we engineer a Pareto improvement?*

Last time we introduced the idea of a co-op to show that making good choices, choices that minimized the opportunity cost among two farmers, we were able to allow more to be possible within the group without needing to work more or to develop better technology. The choices themselves *ARE* the technology.

We talked about self-trades along the PPF and left by asking what exchange rates each farmer would accept from the other farmer. The short answer here is Molly will accept any trade that’s a better deal than her self-trade. 

There's a nice little way to visualize a trade’s exchange rate. Lets pick some **initial endowment**, which is just a fancy name for how much of both goods Molly has. If Molly grows only \_\_ , her initial endowment is all \__. If she traded \_\_ for \_\_ , she would move from \_\_ to \_\_ . If she kept trading at this rate, we can see all her possible endowments after trade.

***Show this in one unit increments on her graph.***

 

We can represent these points with a line. Just like with the PPF, the slope of the line represents the exchange rate. If the trade line lives outside Molly's PPF, then she has more post-trade crops after specialization and trade than she could have on her own in autarky. If the trade line is on the outside of her PPF, she will accept the terms of trade. If the trade line is on the inside of her PPF, she will reject the terms of trade because she could do better by perform a self-trade.

This idea works for any initial endowment. Let’s say Molly doesn’t fully specialize in her comparative advantage. We can think about a trade starting at any point like this point right in the middle. If the trade was better in one direction, she would accept a trade that way. If it were better in the other direction, she would accept a trade *that* way. 

*And I want to add something here or maybe latter about how the two farmers will want to move toward their comparative advantage.*

For a trade to work, however, both sides of the trade must be willing to accept the terms. This example so far has been very nice for Molly. But for the trade to work, Andrew will need to want the deal too. Just like Molly, Andrew's PPF represents his opportunity cost, which is the terms of *his* self-trade. And just like Molly, for Andrew to want to accept the terms of trade, the trade line running through his initial endowment must lie on the outside of his PPF. While this example is very nice for Molly, it is not terms that Andrew will accept.

Andrew could counter offer with terms that are very nice for him. Lets say \_\_ for \_\_ . Again, for the deal to work, both parties must like it better than their self-trade. Like before, however, this trade only benefits one of the two parties, Andrew in this case.

Molly counter offers with a trade that lives somewhere in the middle, say \_\_ for \_\_ . This exchange rate turns out to be better for both Molly and Andrew. This means both parties find the terms of the trade favorable and will accept.

What I've shown here is a small trade. But the goal for both farmers is to do better than the highlighted points in autarky. We can achieve this using Molly's counter offer exchange rate of \_\_ for \_\_ . If they trade \_\_ for \_\_ , both farmers have more of both crops by specializing and trading with each other, and this hasn't required a guild to pull it off. We've simply specialized and traded.

You might be wondering how we found this exchange rate. It turns out we can always find a trade like this by looking at the two farmers' opportunity costs. Look at the opportunity costs for both farmers and compare it to the exchange rate that has worked here. You can see that so long as the exchange rate lives between the two farmers' terms of self-trade, they can be made better off than in autarky.

### Society's PPF

*This extends A3 by showing various not-fully specialized points.*

If the trade line lies below the PPF, the trade doesn't do better than the PPF for the farmer. So we'll only highlight the achievable area made possible by the trade. We also need to realize that not every trade is feasible, though, since the other farmer can only away what they're harvesting. So we constrain the trade line on its upper end as well. This leaves us with a line representing the PPF as well as a line representing the range of possible trades.

When thinking about trades, we have two levers. First, the exchange rate, represented by the slope of the trade line. If the trade is too steep, its great for one farmer and terrible the other. And if its too flat, the same but reversed. So the only trades that are acceptable happen when the exchange rate lies between the opportunity costs of the two farmers.

And second, we can choose how much of each goods the farmers grow. Earlier we showed that Molly has a comparative advantage and spinach and Andrew has a comparative advantage in carrots so each is gonna specialize in each of these two goods. Each farmer specializing according to their comparative advantage, Molly harvests $40$ tons of spinach and Andrew harvests $8$ units of carrots. This is like the co-op solution, but here we're looking for a trade that makes both farmers better off. And it's important to note here that we STILL haven't imposed preferences. All we're looking at is the frontier, and asking if we can find a way to live outside it without changing inputs.

As Molly shifts toward her specialty crop, she's able to trade more of her specialty to Andrew, and Andrew is able to exchange for more of the good not his specialty. But the real magic happens when we move both Molly AND Andrew toward their specialty's, extending the range of possible trades.

At this point it becomes clarifying when we plot the trade lines on one graph for the farmers together.

Returning to the challenge we set out on, specializing, exchanging $C$ carrot for $S$ spinach sets the exchange rate to $X$, which works for both farmers, means both farmers have more of both goods than they had in autarky.

The benefits of trade become more extreme if opportunity cost were greater for either or both of the farmers.

It turns out in both goods both farmers are doing better than they were before. So not only have we improved the group as a whole but we've also improved each individual farmer by choosing the appropriate exchange rate. This isn’t some mathematical sleight of hand. No matter what the original production levels were, as long as both farmers have different productive capabilities, we can always do better for a range of points on the PPF with specialization and trade. We’ve chosen the original values to make the math work out cleanly. But there’s nothing special about them.

One question I'll leave you with: can we always find a trade like this?

We’ve done something extraordinary here. If this doesn’t spark your interest in the field, I’m not sure I can help you! We’ve shown that two types of coordination, the co-op, and trading, not only increase the total harvest, but also can increase the crops for each farmer individually. And we did this simply by realizing that tradeoffs are important, and that exchanges that take advantage of opportunity cost and comparative advantage mean cooperation can make both farmers better off without giving up any harvest in the process. We’ve just put numbers to something that at the outset might have sounded impossible.

Interaction between decision-makers like this is central to what we do in microeconomics. Welcome! We have a lot to do.

Is there some coordination device that would make this possible? The answer here again is YES!

economics broadly is about how we should organize society. while we talk a lot about prices as a coordinating mechanism in markets, as you'll see, the questions we're after are much bigger. the tradition of economics started around the time and place of the industrial revolution, as society (especially but not exclusively) started to transition. in many ways the dissatisfactory European feudalism of the pre-industrial revolution was more of a historical anomaly than the norm. there's a great deal of interesting history of political economy, with markets, exchange, and specialization dating back for thousands of years and in nearly every part of the globe. but in many ways the origin of the study of economics in the way we understand it today began as a question during a historically unprecedented societal transition out of feudal then merchantilist Europe into an expanding industrial revolution: if not feudalism, then what?
