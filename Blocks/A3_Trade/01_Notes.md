### Specialization and Trade

Economics broadly is about how we should organize society. While we talk a lot about prices as a coordinating mechanism in markets, as you'll see, the questions we're after are much bigger. The tradition of economics started around the time and place of the industrial revolution. In many ways the dissatisfaction with European feudalism during the pre-industrial revolution was more of a historical anomaly than the norm. Usually revolutions don’t happen. Political economy has a long and interesting history, with ideas and theory of markets, exchange, and specialization dating back thousands of years and in nearly every part of the globe. But in many ways the origin of the study of economics in the way we understand it today began as a question during a historically unprecedented societal transition out of feudal into a mercantilist Europe and then again into an expanding industrial revolution. The question was if not feudalism, a political system ruled by those holding land, or mercantilism, a political system designed to maximize exports and minimize imports, then what?

One of the first rigorous answers came from the model we started last time, David Ricardo, writing in 1817.

Last time we introduced the idea of a co-op to show that making good choices, choices that minimized the opportunity cost among two farmers, we were able to allow more to be possible within the group without needing to work more or to develop better technology. The choices themselves *ARE* the technology.

We talked about self-trades along the PPF and left by asking what exchange rates each farmer would accept from the other farmer. The short answer here is Molly will accept any trade that’s a better deal than her self-trade. 

There's a nice little way to visualize a trade’s exchange rate. Let's pick some **initial endowment**, which is just a fancy name for how much of both goods Molly has. If Molly grows only spinach, her initial endowment is all spinach, $(0, 40)$. If she traded $6$ spinach for $4$ carrots, she would move from $(0, 40)$ to $(4, 34)$. ~~If she kept trading at this rate, we can see all her possible endowments after trade.~~

For a trade to work, however, both sides of the trade must be willing to accept the terms. This example so far has been very nice for Molly. But for the trade to work, Andrew will need to want the deal too. Just like Molly, Andrew's PPF represents his opportunity cost, which is the terms of *his* self-trade. And just like Molly, for Andrew to want to accept the terms of trade, ~~the trade line running through his initial endowment must lie on the outside of his PPF~~ {++the trade must be a better deal than his self-trade++}. <!-- ED: pointwise rewording with the line parked — recycled from your "better deal than her self-trade" line above; adjust freely. --> While this example is very nice for Molly, it is not terms that Andrew will accept.

Andrew could counter offer with terms that are very nice for him. Let's say $5$ spinach for $1$ carrot. Again, for the deal to work, both parties must like it better than their self-trade. Like before, however, this trade only benefits one of the two parties, Andrew in this case.

Molly counter offers with a trade that lives somewhere in the middle, say $3$ spinach for $1$ carrot. This exchange rate turns out to be better for both Molly and Andrew. This means both parties find the terms of the trade favorable and will accept. Trading like this is what we call a **Pareto improvement**, and it's something we love to find in the world.

What I've shown here is a small trade. But the goal for both farmers is to do better than the highlighted points in autarky. We can achieve this using Molly's counter offer exchange rate of $3$ spinach for $1$ carrot. If they trade $3.5$ carrots for $10.5$ spinach, both farmers have more of both crops by specializing and trading with each other, and this hasn't required a co-op to pull it off. You might be wondering how we found this exchange rate. It turns out we can always find a trade like this by looking at the two farmers' opportunity costs. Look at the opportunity costs for both farmers and compare it to the exchange rate that has worked here. You can see that so long as the exchange rate lives between the two farmers' terms of self-trade, they can be made better off than in autarky.

Growing a carrot herself costs Molly $4$ spinach but buying one costs $3$. Every carrot she grows herself she is paying the expensive price. So instead of growing them she grows none and trades her spinach for them at the cheaper price instead. The same is true for Andrew, just on the other side of the trade. Growing a spinach himself costs him $\frac{1}{2}$ a carrot, while trading for one costs him $\frac{1}{3}$. So he grows all carrots. Both farmers go all the way to their corners.

As Molly shifts toward her specialty crop, she's able to trade more of her specialty to Andrew, and Andrew is able to exchange for more of the good not his specialty. But the real magic happens when we move both Molly AND Andrew toward their specialties, extending the range of possible trades.

Like I mentioned last time, with two goods and two farmers like this, each farmer will always have a comparative advantage in one of the goods, so long as the farmers don't have the same opportunity costs. This means, we can always find an exchange rate that works. You might be able to realize this by thinking about the fact that opportunity costs are reciprocals.

This isn’t some mathematical sleight of hand. No matter what the original production levels were, as long as both farmers have different productive capabilities, we can always do better for a range of points on the PPF with specialization and trade. We’ve chosen the original values to make the math work out cleanly. But there’s nothing special about them.

We’ve done something extraordinary here. If this doesn’t spark your interest in the field, I’m not sure I can help you! We’ve shown that two types of coordination, the co-op, and trading, not only increase the total harvest, but also can increase the crops for each farmer individually. And we did this simply by realizing that tradeoffs are important, and that exchanges that take advantage of opportunity cost and comparative advantage mean cooperation can make both farmers better off without giving up any harvest in the process. We’ve just put numbers to something that at the outset might have sounded impossible.
